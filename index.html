    Oh No, The Phones
    Mom’s phone number was 620. If a friend wanted to reach her on her family’s farm just outside the rural Missouri town of Alma (pop. 400, more or less), they had to buzz the operator at the Alma Telephone Company and ask for the Corders or say, “Give me 620, please.” The operator, who probably knew by heart which circuit the Corders were on, would ring line No. 6 with the prescribed sequence: two long rings, zero short rings. The phones in 10 or so households all along the party line would let loose, with the ring pattern signaling to the other farmsteads that the call was for the Corder household. But anyone who wanted to pick it up could—and sometimes did. “You could usually tell when somebody else was on the line,” Mom said. Most often that somebody was Grandma Dieckhoff, who lived one house down the line with her son’s family. The ever-present possibility of eavesdroppers disciplined Mom’s high school chatter—except when it supercharged it: “We sometimes exaggerated a little to give her something to talk about.” Mom remembers precisely when her parents finally got off the party line, much later than most of the U.S.: December 1972, her freshman year at the University of Missouri. Late-adopting Alma Telephone skipped over rotary phones altogether, heading straight to direct dial.     Voyager 1, A Day Of Light Away
    Humans have trouble with inhuman distances. Miles are about the largest comprehensible unit of measurement we have, because we can understand miles. Watering hole is in a mile? No problem, we’ll be there soon. Aurochs herd is 30 miles away? OK, our hunting party won't walk that in a day, but it’s doable. Miles are a good measure for us, and on the scale of the universe, absolutely useless. The Sun is about 93 million miles from Earth. Can that possibly be spatially encompassed by a human mind that evolved to think in terms of footsteps? The nearest star after that is about 25 trillion miles away . We have the words, but they blur into a meaningless litany of zeroes and -illions. How big is the observable universe? 546,710,000,000,000,000,000,000 miles across, best we can tell. What does that mean to us? Nothing. I submit that while it’s less accurate, it’s more truthful to say simply that the universe is “very big.” Enter light. The fastest thing there is, light travels through a vacuum at 186,000 miles per second, which gives us a helpful benchmark for talking about cosmic distances. (And, in a universe where everything is flying around in different directions, light travels at the same speed from any reference point, which is not intuitive but very helpful. Good job, Einstein.) So 186,000 miles is called a light-second. A light-year, naturally, is the distance it takes light to travel in a year. We can say that Proxima Centauri, alluded to in the previous paragraph, is 25 trillion miles away, or we can say that it’s about four light-years away. Guess which astronomers prefer.  ( 21 min )

    Lars Ingebrigtsen: OK, OK, I’ll just use tmdb
    After last night’s post, I realised I was probably just getting too invested in somehow using imdb for the “who’s that actor, anyway?” command for my Emacs-based video player. Not only is it an extremely fragile setup that’s gonna stop working, but it’s also very slow — it takes half a minute to return the actor profile images. So I just implemented a tmdb API shim and then I ask it for the actor profile photo instead. tmdb doesn’t have as large a data base, but… The API is free (for non-commercial use) and is extremely easy to use. Things are now faster: The main wait is from the response from the LLM — I’m using gemini-3.8-flash. I’ve tried gemini-3.1-flash-lite, and it’s much faster… but gives the wrong result most of the time. No, that’s not Roy Scheider, Gemini! So now the setup is less Rube Goldberguesque. Sort of: I hit a key on my li’l wireless keyboard. It’s picked up by a Python script that listens for evdev events. It uses emacsclient to notify the Emacs running on the TV machine. Emacs there tells mpv to do a screenshot. Emacs waits until the screenshot arrives on disc. Emacs sends it to Gemini and says “who dis? JSON please”. Gemini responds and Emacs then creates an SVG overlay with the actor name. rsvg-convert converts to PNG and then imagemagick convert to BGRA. Emacs asks mpv to display it. Emacs asks tmdb for the actor profile URL and fetches the image. If it gets a response, it then recreates the svg with the image embedded. (repeat 8-9). Couldn’t be simpler.  ( 4 min )
